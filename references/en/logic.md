# Logic, Argument and Evidence

English companion to [the Chinese guide](../logic.md), which includes sources. Evaluate reasoning and premises separately; do not use labels as a substitute for explanation.

## Deduction
- **Use:** Derive a necessary conclusion from explicit rules or examine validity.
- **Do:** State premises, quantifiers and conclusion; check whether the premises could all be true while the conclusion is false, then verify the premises independently.
- **Limits:** Validity does not establish true premises; clarify ambiguities.

## Induction
- **Use:** Generalize a trend or regularity from observations.
- **Do:** Define population, sampling, sample size and counterexamples; assess bias and changes over time and give a proportionate provisional conclusion.
- **Limits:** Consistent observations do not guarantee a general rule; small samples may not represent the population.

## Abduction
- **Use:** Generate and compare explanations for observed facts.
- **Do:** List candidates, compare evidence, assumptions and counterexamples, and design observations or tests to distinguish them.
- **Limits:** The best current explanation is not proven; omitted alternatives matter.

## Necessary and sufficient conditions
- **Use:** Examine “if,” “only if” and related conditions in rules, contracts or arguments.
- **Do:** Express “if P then Q” as P → Q: P is sufficient for Q and Q is necessary for P. Look for cases with P true and Q false.
- **Limits:** Do not reverse the implication or infer P from Q; ordinary-language conditions can be ambiguous.

## Modus ponens and modus tollens
- **Use:** Verify basic conditional inferences.
- **Do:** From P → Q and P infer Q; from P → Q and not-Q infer not-P. State the rule and premises.
- **Limits:** Affirming the consequent or denying the antecedent is invalid; real-world rules may admit exceptions.

## Counterexample
- **Use:** Test universal claims such as “all” or “never.”
- **Do:** State the precise scope and find a verified case satisfying the premise but not the conclusion.
- **Limits:** One valid counterexample defeats a universal claim but does not prove the opposite universal.

## Proof by contradiction
- **Use:** Test a formal proposition against accepted assumptions.
- **Do:** In a specified logic, assume the proposition false and derive a contradiction; inspect each inference and whether the assumption can be rejected.
- **Limits:** Results depend on assumptions and logical rules; an undesirable consequence is not a logical contradiction.

## Argument mapping
- **Use:** Clarify complex claims, supporting reasons, evidence and objections.
- **Do:** Map conclusions, reasons, evidence, implicit assumptions, counterarguments, responses and sources.
- **Limits:** Clear structure is not evidence of truth; do not attribute invented premises to another person.

## Steelmanning
- **Use:** Assess a disputed claim without attacking a weaker distortion.
- **Do:** Reconstruct its strongest faithful version based on the person's evidence and context, then assess support, objections and remaining disagreements.
- **Limits:** Do not attribute invented claims to others or assume all positions have equal evidence.

## Fallacy and equivocation checks
- **Use:** Examine potential straw men, false dilemmas, ad hominem, circularity or shifting meanings.
- **Do:** Identify the specific reasoning, explain the defect in context and suggest what evidence or correction is needed.
- **Limits:** A label alone is not an analysis; an invalid argument does not automatically make its conclusion false.

## Bayesian updating
- **Use:** Reassess hypotheses after new evidence.
- **Do:** State supported prior beliefs or ranges, compare the likelihood of evidence under alternatives and update, with sensitivity analysis.
- **Limits:** Use qualitative comparisons when numerical priors or likelihoods lack support; avoid double-counting dependent evidence.

## Base rates
- **Use:** Assess likelihood using both population frequencies and individual evidence.
- **Do:** Find a suitable population and time period, then update with case-specific information; use natural frequencies where helpful.
- **Limits:** Population averages may not apply to a particular individual; consider selection and conditional probabilities.

## Falsification and competing hypotheses
- **Use:** Make a broad claim testable and compare alternative explanations.
- **Do:** Identify observations expected under each hypothesis and checks that could weaken or overturn it.
- **Limits:** One failed replication does not alone defeat an entire theory; measurement and auxiliary assumptions may fail too.

## Correlation, causation and confounding
- **Use:** Determine whether an action caused an observed change.
- **Do:** Specify the causal question and comparison; consider common causes, reverse causation and selection bias, and use feasible experiments or quasi-experiments.
- **Limits:** Correlation and temporal order alone do not establish causality; state unresolved alternative explanations.

## Counterfactual comparison
- **Use:** Assess an action against no action or an alternative.
- **Do:** Define the intervention, outcome, period and population; choose an appropriate comparison and state identification assumptions.
- **Limits:** The same subject's alternative outcome at the same time is unobservable; an imagined scenario alone is not a credible counterfactual.

## Survivorship and selection bias
- **Use:** Evidence contains only successes, active users or respondents.
- **Do:** Examine how observations entered the sample, seek data about failures and nonrespondents, and compare with the target population.
- **Limits:** Missing cases cannot be automatically reconstructed; replacement samples may also be biased.
